# Mass timber moves into mid-rise offices

Developers in several markets are choosing engineered timber for buildings of
eight to twelve storeys, a range that was concrete-and-steel by default a decade
ago. The driver is schedule: a timber superstructure is prefabricated off site
and assembled in weeks, and the reduced weight shrinks the foundations.

## The cost picture

On a direct materials basis, timber is still more expensive than a concrete
frame in most regions. The saving is indirect. A shorter build means less time
paying for the site, the crane, and the financing, and an earlier rent roll.

- Foundation volume typically falls by a fifth to a third.
- Trade stacking on site is lower, so fewer delays cascade.
- Fit-out can begin on lower floors while the frame rises above.

Where the local supply chain is thin, the premium can erase the schedule saving,
so the economics are regional rather than universal.

## Fire and insurance

Engineered timber chars predictably and retains structural capacity for a
defined period, which is how it satisfies code. Insurers were initially cautious
and priced the buildings as a higher risk. As a portfolio of completed projects
has accumulated without incident, several carriers have moved timber mid-rise
into their standard commercial band.

## Regulatory state

Building codes in the relevant markets now permit timber to the heights
described, subject to sprinklers and to encapsulation of some connections. A few
jurisdictions still require a case-by-case engineering review above a threshold
height, which adds time to the approval.

## What to watch

The constraint over the next few years is fabrication capacity, not demand.
Plants that press and mill the panels are capital-intensive and slow to build,
and order books at the established mills already run months ahead.
